Dr. Philippa Hardman has written an [excellent article](https://drphilippahardman.substack.com/p/beyond-infographics-how-to-use-nano) on using Google's Nano Banana AI image generation tool to support learning. The piece outlines six evidence-based use cases that go far beyond simple infographics: visualization, analogy, worked examples, contrasting cases, elaboration, and generation. Each strategy is grounded in decades of cognitive and educational research, and Hardman provides concrete prompts that instructional designers can immediately put to use.

The article is particularly valuable because it moves beyond the typical "AI can make pretty pictures" conversation to show how Nano Banana's unique capabilities—multimodal inputs, persistent visual identity, and instructional terminology awareness—can operationalize proven learning strategies at scale. This is the kind of thoughtful, research-informed approach to AI tools that the field needs.

However, the article also reinforces a critical lesson I've learned from my own experiences with AI: **often it's close enough, but it's critical to review the outputs carefully.**

Take, for example, the worked example image that Hardman includes in her article—a 5-step visual guide for tying a bowline knot. The bowline is a fundamental knot used in countless situations, from sailing to rescue operations to everyday tasks. When tied correctly, it's reliable and secure. When tied incorrectly, it can fail catastrophically—a serious concern when lives depend on it.

The Nano Banana-generated image in the article, while visually polished and well-structured, contains errors in the knot-tying sequence. This isn't a criticism of Hardman's work (she's using it as an example of the tool's capabilities, not as a knot-tying tutorial), but rather a reminder that even when AI produces something that looks professional and well-organized, domain expertise and careful review remain essential.

This "close enough" phenomenon is something I encounter regularly with AI tools. They can produce impressive outputs that are 80-90% correct, but that remaining 10-20% can be critical. Whether it's a knot-tying sequence, a technical diagram, a historical timeline, or a scientific explanation, the final review by someone with domain knowledge is not optional—it's essential.

Hardman's article is excellent precisely because it shows how to use AI tools thoughtfully and strategically. But it also serves as a reminder that these tools are amplifiers of human expertise, not replacements for it. The six strategies she outlines are powerful precisely because they're grounded in decades of research on how humans actually learn. The AI tool makes them more accessible and scalable, but the underlying learning science—and the critical review process—remains firmly in human hands.

So yes, use Nano Banana to create worked examples, visualizations, and contrasting cases. But always review the outputs with the same rigor you'd apply to any instructional material. Because when it comes to teaching and learning, "close enough" isn't good enough.
